You have content, an advert and a page to send people to. But what happens after someone clicks, asks a question or leaves an email address?

A funnel plan makes those next steps explicit. It tells your team what the prospective customer needs to understand and who is responsible for helping them move forward.

This guide focuses on building that journey for a coaching offer or online store.

## What is a sales funnel in practical terms?

For this guide, a sales funnel is the planned route from an initial interaction to a buying decision and relevant follow-up. It connects the message, destination, action and response your business will provide.

Write the journey in ordinary language:

- Someone encounters the offer.
- They learn enough to assess it.
- They take a relevant next step.
- Your business answers remaining questions.
- They decide whether to buy.
- You provide the agreed customer experience.

Use it to find unclear handovers. For a closer look at the concept, read [What Is a Marketing Funnel?](/blog/what-is-a-marketing-funnel/).

## What should you decide before building a funnel?

Decide what you are selling, who it is for and what action you want a suitable prospective customer to take. Resolve the essential offer details before writing pages and emails.

Prepare:

1. The problem or need the offer addresses.
2. The product or programme details.
3. The price and payment terms.
4. The intended customer and any eligibility conditions.
5. Delivery, availability and relevant policies.
6. The next action and its owner.

If these details are unsettled, record them as open decisions. Do not let a designer or automation tool silently decide them.

For example, an application funnel needs a definition of a suitable applicant. A product funnel needs accurate stock, fulfilment and buying information.

## How should coaching and e-commerce funnels differ?

Design the funnel around the buying decision and delivery model of the offer. A coaching application and a product checkout need different information, actions and follow-up.

Use this comparison to plan the work:

| Decision | Coaching offer | E-commerce offer |
|---|---|---|
| Suitability | Who should apply? | Who is the product for? |
| Details | Programme, support and delivery | Features, fit, variants and availability |
| Next step | Application, call or purchase | Product selection and checkout |
| Follow-up | Questions, suitability and booking | Product questions and order support |
| Capacity | Places and service delivery | Stock and fulfilment |
| Review | Qualified applications and enrolments | Completed orders and contribution |

These are suggested routes, not requirements. Choose the simplest journey that fits your actual offer and the decision your customer needs to make.

## How do you map the customer journey?

Map each intended action, the information needed beforehand and the response that follows. Include both the customer-facing step and the internal responsibility.

Create a worksheet with these columns:

- Entry point.
- Message or question.
- Destination.
- Action requested.
- Information captured.
- Team or system responsible.
- What happens next.

For a coach, trace the path from a piece of content to the programme page, application and response. For a store, trace the path from the product message to product selection, checkout and confirmation.

Then inspect the handovers. Does the page explain what the content promised? Does an applicant know when and how to expect a response? Identify missing decisions before building the system.

## What should the landing page include?

Include the information needed to assess the offer and take the intended next step. Keep the page consistent with the message that sends people there.

A useful brief covers:

- What is being offered.
- Who it is suitable for.
- What the customer receives.
- Price or the process for obtaining a quote.
- Verified evidence, where available.
- Relevant conditions and policies.
- A clear call to action.

Write answers to the questions your sales team actually hears. Do not invent urgency, testimonials or numerical outcomes to make the page feel complete.

If a form or checkout belongs on the page, test it as a customer. Confirm the fields, instructions, payment information and message shown after submission.

## How should you capture and qualify leads?

Ask for information that has a defined purpose in the sales process. Decide how each answer will inform follow-up before adding the question to a form.

For a coaching application, you might need to understand the person's goal, current situation and suitability. Include budget or timing questions only where they help you decide the next step.

Create a response plan for:

1. Suitable applicants.
2. People who need more information.
3. People who are outside the offer's scope.
4. Incomplete applications.

Explain what happens after submission. Avoid inviting an application if no one owns the response. Document data handling and permission requirements using guidance relevant to your market.

## What should your follow-up sequence say?

Write follow-up that delivers what was promised, answers relevant questions and explains the next step. Plan the messages around the person's action and your real process.

For a coaching enquiry, a proposed sequence might:

- Confirm receipt or deliver the requested resource.
- Explain suitability and programme structure.
- Answer a common concern.
- Share an approved, verified example.
- Invite the relevant application or booking action.

For a store, plan the information appropriate to the product and customer action. Verify the capabilities and permissions of the tools you use before implementing automated messages.

## What should you test before launch?

Test the entire intended journey from entry point to confirmation and internal response. Record the result of each check and fix broken or unclear steps before directing people through it.

Work through:

1. The link in the content or ad.
2. The destination on relevant devices.
3. The offer details and current terms.
4. Form submission or checkout.
5. Confirmation and delivery messages.
6. Team notifications and follow-up.
7. The records used for reporting.

Use test data where appropriate and avoid creating misleading real orders or customer communications. Confirm how the team distinguishes testing from actual activity.

Ask someone unfamiliar with the build to explain the next step after using it. Their questions can help identify wording that needs clarification.

## How do you measure funnel performance?

Track the actions you defined and compare progression between stages using consistent records. Review the business outcome alongside the intermediate steps.

For a coaching offer, agree how to record applications, qualified applicants, booked calls, attendance and enrolments. For an online store, agree how to review product visits, checkout activity, orders and relevant costs.

Where measurement depends on platform tools, check implementation and the current documentation before interpreting the figures.

Avoid assigning a cause from one number alone. If applications are unsuitable, examine the message, offer and qualification criteria. If sales are weak, investigate what happens after the application as well.

Keep a decision log showing the issue, change made and evidence you will review next.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a complicated funnel?

Build the simplest journey that supports your offer and provides the information a customer needs. Add steps only when you can explain their purpose and manage them properly. A clear page, action and follow-up process may be the appropriate scope to discuss for your business.

### Can a funnel guarantee sales?

Treat sales as the intended outcome rather than a guaranteed result. Define the offer, qualification, measurement and operational responsibilities, then review what happens at each stage. Ask any provider to explain its claims and the evidence behind them before agreeing to a performance promise.
